The grammar — XLRM (Lempert / RAND), operationalized literally
Two sets of dials, one engine, one readout
XLRM is usually a workshop checklist. Here it is enforced as a type system: every one of the 24 extracted parameters carries a tag, and the tag decides who may move it. The whole architecture of the workbench is in the diagram below.
The machine — how the four parts relate
Samsung chooses L. The world owns X, and you can't put odds on it. The same engine R turns every X × L pairing into M — so the goal is the lever-setting that holds up across every world, not the single run that scores best.
A full column of X values is a world; a full column of L values is a strategy. Read left to right: a world meets a strategy inside the engine, and the only place a number is ever allowed to appear is M. The same R is reused for every world × strategy pair — 20 runs, one engine.

The safety rail — confabulation becomes a type error

The most seductive analytical failure is a story where a strategy quietly improves the weather, or a scenario quietly fixes the plan. Because every dial is tagged with its owner, those stories are unwriteable — the schema rejects them the way a compiler rejects a type error.

a world tries to shrink the constellation  →  world.set("P1", 1000)
✕ REJECTED P1 (constellation size) is L-tagged — Samsung-owned. Worlds may move only X.
a strategy tries to raise the sovereignty premium  →  strategy.set("P19", 0.30)
✕ REJECTED P19 (sovereignty premium) is X-tagged — world-owned. Strategies may move only L.
The LLM works upstream of the rail: it frames the worlds and proposes which X-dials they move. The numbers themselves come from the ~200-line engine, every time. That split is what makes every figure in this app auditable.
